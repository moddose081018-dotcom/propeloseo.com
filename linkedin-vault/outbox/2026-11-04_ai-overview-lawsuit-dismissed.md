The same judge who ruled Google illegally maintains a search monopoly just said AI Overviews aren't an antitrust violation.

Let that sit for a second.

Judge Amit Mehta dismissed Chegg's and Penske Media's lawsuits on September 30. A 41-page opinion. Both cases thrown out.

The core of his ruling: "An expectation is not an agreement."

Translation — publishers hoped Google would keep sending traffic. Google never promised that. Hoping someone will keep doing something doesn't make it a contract.

This is the legal reality SEOs need to absorb:

There is no enforceable right to search referral traffic. There never was.

Google can put AI Overviews on every query. They can answer every question without a click. And the courts just confirmed there's no legal mechanism to stop them.

I know the reaction will be "but copyright" or "but licensing." The ruling specifically says those questions remain open. Content scraping and fair use are separate fights.

But the antitrust path — the argument that Google owes publishers traffic — is done.

What this means practically:

If your traffic strategy depends on Google choosing to send clicks, you're building on someone else's goodwill. Not on a right. Not on a contract. On an expectation.

We've been telling clients for months: diversify away from pure search dependency. This ruling makes that urgency concrete.

The publishers who survive this shift won't be the ones who sue. They'll be the ones who build direct audiences, email lists, and products that don't need a search referral to generate revenue.

What am I missing here? If you think there's still a legal path to force Google to share traffic, I'd genuinely love to hear the argument.
