"More content = more traffic" was the SEO playbook for a decade. The data says it stopped being true.

We've been running more aggressive content audits this year. The same pattern keeps showing up.

Hundreds of posts. Most ranking for something. Almost none generating clicks.

MarketBetter published the clearest case recently. They deleted 161 AI-generated blog posts. Those posts had earned 570,000 impressions in 90 days — and 521 clicks. A 0.091% CTR.

Their surviving hand-written content? 0.280% CTR. More than 3x higher.

That gap is what we're finding on client sites too. AI-scaled content earns impressions. It rarely earns clicks.

Seer Interactive ran the largest documented prune — roughly 14,000 pages removed. It reversed a multi-year traffic decline. Inflow cut underperforming content and saw 104% more organic sessions.

But IBM's case is the most honest. They removed 1,000+ pages and traffic stayed flat. The team called it a win.

That's the part people miss. Pruning isn't always about gaining traffic. Sometimes it's about stopping the dilution.

The fix isn't always deletion either. Google's own guidance calls it a "last resort." Sometimes three thin posts become one thorough one.

But the conversation always starts the same way: "We spent money on that content."

And that's exactly why the bloat compounds.

Have you had to make the case for deleting content from a client's site? What finally convinced the stakeholder?
